# Acorn-SVD: Spectral-Geometry-Constrained Adversarial Training for Neural Representation Learning

**Technical Research Progress Report**

**Dataset:** C-CO12 Neural Recording Session  
**Framework:** Acorn-SVD (Modified CEBRA)  
**Experiment Date:** October 16, 2026  
**Computational Platform:** NVIDIA H200 GPU, EPFL RunAI  
**Experimental Scope:** 5 adversarial training methods × 3 random seeds = 15 trained encoder–decoder pipelines

---

## Abstract

This report presents an experimental investigation of spectral-geometry-constrained adversarial training for CEBRA-based neural representation learning. The proposed framework, **Acorn-SVD**, uses singular value decomposition (SVD) of neural activity windows to construct adversarial perturbations in geometrically defined subspaces. The central motivation is to investigate whether controlling perturbation directions relative to the dominant singular structure of neural population activity can improve the quality and robustness of learned neural representations.

Five training strategies were compared: conventional projected gradient descent (PGD), spectral-gap-constrained PGD (SVD-Gap), tangent-space-constrained PGD (SVD-Tangent), gap-budget-only PGD (SVD-Gap-Budget), and subspace-projected PGD without adaptive budget constraints (SVD-Subspace).

Experiments were performed using the C-CO12 neural recording session, comprising 86 neural input features and six behavioral output variables. Each training strategy was evaluated with three random seeds (42, 43, and 44), producing 15 completed experiments. All encoders were trained for 3,000 iterations using the same architecture and optimization settings. Behavioral decoding was assessed using a two-layer MLP, with additional evaluations under adversarial attacks and simulated neural recording corruptions.

The main findings are:

- **SVD-Subspace and SVD-Gap preserved the selected dominant singular structure to approximately numerical precision**, with maximum recorded relative singular-value errors near 1.19 × 10⁻⁷.
- **SVD-Tangent achieved the highest average clean decoding R²**, reaching 0.6920 compared with 0.6811 for conventional PGD, although its performance varied substantially across seeds.
- **SVD-Subspace showed a more consistent clean decoding improvement**, outperforming PGD in all three seeds and achieving mean R² = 0.6908.
- **SVD-Tangent substantially improved robustness to simulated loss of 30% of recorded neurons**, increasing mean R² from 0.4351 (PGD) to 0.5357.
- However, **SVD-Tangent exhibited a major loss of robustness against unrestricted PGD**, with R² decreasing to 0.2079 compared with 0.5712 for the PGD-trained baseline.
- The additional computational cost of the spectral methods was approximately 19–21% compared with conventional PGD.

Overall, the results provide encouraging evidence that spectral geometry can be used to construct precisely controlled adversarial perturbations and that different spectral constraints induce different forms of robustness. However, improvements in spectral preservation do not automatically imply improved robustness against unrestricted adversarial attacks.

## 1. Research Objective

CEBRA learns low-dimensional representations of neural population activity through contrastive learning. Such representations can be used to decode behavioral variables and study the structure of neural dynamics.

A central concern is the sensitivity of these representations to changes in neural activity. Conventional adversarial training exposes a neural encoder to perturbations that maximize a training loss within a predefined norm budget. However, these perturbations are not explicitly constrained to preserve important spectral characteristics of neural population activity.

The Acorn-SVD project explores an alternative: constructing adversarial perturbations using the singular geometry of the neural activity itself.

The study addresses three research questions:

**RQ1 — Spectral preservation:** Can adversarial perturbations be constructed that preserve the dominant singular values and singular subspaces of neural activity windows?

**RQ2 — Representation quality:** Does spectral-geometry-constrained adversarial training improve downstream behavioral decoding compared with conventional PGD?

**RQ3 — Robustness:** Do different spectral constraints improve resistance to adversarial attacks or realistic recording disturbances, including neural unit loss, Poisson resampling, and gain drift?

An additional objective is to separate the contributions of **perturbation direction** and **adaptive perturbation magnitude** through controlled ablation experiments.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Neural Activity Representation

Each neural activity window is represented by a matrix:

\[
X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T},
\]

where \(N\) denotes the number of neurons and \(T\) represents the temporal window length.

The temporal mean is subtracted before computing the singular value decomposition:

\[
X_c = U\Sigma V^\top.
\]

Here, \(U\) and \(V\) contain the left and right singular vectors, respectively, and \(\Sigma\) contains the singular values in descending order.

A protected rank \(k\) is selected for each window based on the largest spectral gap within a predefined rank range:

\[
k=\operatorname*{argmax}_{j\in\{1,\ldots,8\}}
(\sigma_j-\sigma_{j+1}).
\]

The corresponding spectral gap is:

\[
g_k=\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1}.
\]

This construction makes the protected rank and spectral-gap budget adaptive to the input window.

### 2.2 SVD Subspace Projection

Let \(G\) denote the gradient used to construct an adversarial perturbation.

The projection onto the orthogonal complement of the dominant left and right singular subspaces is:

\[
G_{\perp}
=
(I-U_kU_k^\top)G(I-V_kV_k^\top).
\]

This construction eliminates gradient components associated with the protected singular directions.

Under the relevant spectral-gap condition, perturbations restricted to this complementary subspace preserve the selected dominant singular values and subspaces of the centered activity matrix.

### 2.3 Spectral-Gap-Adaptive Perturbation Budget

For the SVD-Gap method, the allowed perturbation budget for window \(i\) is:

\[
\epsilon_i
=
\min\left(
\epsilon_{\max},
\gamma(\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1})
\right),
\]

where:

\[
\epsilon_{\max}=3.0,\qquad \gamma=0.9.
\]

Unlike conventional PGD, where every window has the same maximum budget, this formulation restricts perturbation magnitude according to the spectral structure of each window.

The purpose is to keep perturbations below a sufficient threshold for preserving the selected dominant singular structure.

### 2.4 Tangent-Space Perturbations

A second spectral construction uses the complementary tangent-space projection:

\[
G_T=G-G_{\perp}.
\]

This projection selects perturbation directions associated with the tangent space of the rank-\(k\) matrix manifold.

For the SVD-Tangent method, the budget is:

\[
\epsilon_i
=
\min\left(
3.0,
\frac{\gamma}{2}(\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1})
\right).
\]

With \(\gamma=0.9\), this becomes:

\[
\epsilon_i=\min(3.0,0.45g_k).
\]

Unlike the complementary-subspace construction, tangent-space perturbations are allowed to modify the leading singular structure. The spectral-gap condition limits their magnitude to control changes in singular values and prevent closure of the selected spectral gap under the stated mathematical assumptions.

It is important to distinguish a *tangent-space-constrained perturbation* from a perturbation that remains exactly on a nonlinear rank-\(k\) manifold.

### 2.5 Evaluated Training Strategies

Five methods were implemented and evaluated:

| Method | Perturbation direction | Perturbation budget |
|---|---|---|
| **PGD** | Unrestricted gradient | Fixed ε = 3 |
| **SVD-Gap** | Complement of dominant singular subspaces | min(3, 0.9 × gap) |
| **SVD-Tangent** | Tangent space of dominant singular structure | min(3, 0.45 × gap) |
| **SVD-Gap-Budget** | Unrestricted gradient | min(3, 0.9 × gap) |
| **SVD-Subspace** | Complement of dominant singular subspaces | Fixed ε = 3 |

The SVD-Gap-Budget and SVD-Subspace methods are ablations designed to separate the effects of the adaptive budget and subspace projection.

All attacks use L2-constrained projected gradient optimization on neural input windows.

Following encoder training, a separate MLP was trained on the clean training embeddings generated by that encoder.

Decoder architecture:

`Linear(64, 64) → LayerNorm(64) → ReLU → Dropout(0.4) → Linear(64, 6)`

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Hidden dimension | 64 |
| Output dimensions | 6 |
| Dropout | 0.4 |

| Learning rate | 0.001 |
| Training epochs | 2,500 |
| Objective | Mean Squared Error |
| Evaluation metric | Mean R² across all six behavioral outputs |

The decoder was initialized using a controlled seed derived from the encoder seed and evaluated with dropout disabled.

The primary reported decoding score is the arithmetic mean of the six individual R² values.

### 3.4 Experimental Runs

The following design was used:

- Five adversarial training strategies.
- Three random seeds per strategy.
- A total of 15 independently trained encoder–decoder pipelines.
- 3,000 encoder iterations per pipeline.
- 2,500 decoder epochs per pipeline.

No non-adversarial clean CEBRA baseline was included in this experiment.

### 3.5 Adversarial Robustness Evaluation

After training, both the encoder and decoder were frozen.

Adversarial attacks were then generated against the combined encoder–decoder prediction pipeline using prediction MSE as the attack objective.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Evaluation norm | L2 |
| Maximum evaluation ε | 3.0 |
| Evaluation PGD steps | 20 |
| Evaluation step size | 0.375 |
| Evaluation attack batch size | 2,048 |
| Evaluation seed | 1234 |
| Number of validation windows | 4,508 |
| Attack objective | Maximize behavioral prediction MSE |

Three attack types were evaluated:

1. Unrestricted PGD.
2. SVD-Gap-constrained PGD.
3. SVD-Tangent-constrained PGD.

The frozen-model evaluation also included the unperturbed validation windows.

The measured average effective spectral attack budgets were:

| Evaluation attack | Mean allowed perturbation budget |
|---|---:|
| Unrestricted PGD | Maximum ε = 3.000 |
| SVD-Gap | 2.1063 |
| SVD-Tangent | 1.0973 |

These differences are essential when interpreting robustness results: scores obtained under different attack families are not directly comparable as measures of attack strength.

### 3.6 Simulated Neural Recording Corruptions

To assess robustness beyond gradient-based adversarial perturbations, the following corruptions were applied to the validation recordings:

| Corruption | Implementation |
|---|---|
| Poisson resampling | Poisson samples drawn using nonnegative neural activity values as rates |
| 10% neuron dropout | Randomly selected neural features set to zero across the validation sequence |
| 30% neuron dropout | Randomly selected neural features set to zero across the validation sequence |
| Gain drift | Independent multiplicative per-neuron gain factors |
 
For the 86-neuron recording, the dropout conditions correspond to approximately 9 and 26 removed units.

Gain drift used multiplicative factors sampled as:

\[
g_n=\exp(z_n),\qquad z_n\sim\mathcal N(0,0.3^2).
\]

Thus, the gain-drift test corresponds to log-normal multiplicative variation rather than a fixed ±30% change.

Each corruption result was averaged over **five random draws** using a controlled evaluation random seed.

No retraining or decoder adaptation was performed on corrupted validation data.

All reported values are **mean ± sample standard deviation** across the three training seeds unless otherwise specified.

Higher R² indicates better decoding performance.

### 7.1 Effective Adversarial Budgets

| Method | Mean perturbation L2 norm | Mean allowed ε | Fraction gap-limited | Mean protected rank k |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| PGD | 2.9974 | 3.0000 | — | — |
| SVD-Gap | 2.2322 | 2.2342 | 80.28% | 1.4381 |
| SVD-Tangent | 1.1787 | 1.1822 | 99.93% | 1.4380 |
| SVD-Gap-Budget | 2.2328 | 2.2344 | 80.27% | 1.4377 |
| SVD-Subspace | 2.9970 | 3.0000 | 0.00% | 1.4378 |

Conventional PGD and SVD-Subspace used almost the entire available L2 perturbation budget.

SVD-Gap and SVD-Gap-Budget used nearly identical average effective budgets, around 2.234.

SVD-Tangent used a substantially smaller average budget, approximately 1.182.

Nearly every SVD-Tangent training window was constrained by the spectral-gap condition.

The mean protected rank was approximately 1.44 for all spectral methods.

This indicates that the protected spectral structure generally involved one or two dominant singular directions.

### 7.2 Implications for Fair Comparison

Although all methods were configured with a maximum epsilon of 3, they were not exposed to equally large perturbations during training.

This distinction is particularly important for interpreting SVD-Tangent.

Its poor robustness against unrestricted PGD may result from its restricted attack geometry, its smaller effective perturbation magnitude, or both.

Likewise, improvements in clean decoding could potentially arise from differences in perturbation strength rather than spectral preservation alone.

A necessary additional control would be conventional PGD training with smaller fixed budgets, particularly approximately:

- ε = 2.23, matching the mean SVD-Gap training budget.
- ε = 1.18, matching the mean SVD-Tangent training budget.

These are useful matched-mean controls, although matching a single average epsilon does not exactly reproduce the per-window adaptive budget distribution.

## 8. Spectral Preservation Results

### 8.1 Spectral Diagnostics

The following values summarize training-time spectral diagnostics over the three seeds.

The maximum-error metrics are computed as maxima within each training run and subsequently summarized across seeds. They are not exhaustive maxima over all possible input perturbations.

| Method | Relative top-k singular-value error | Left-subspace error | Right-subspace error | Mean spectral drift |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| PGD | 0.082917 | 0.528578 | 0.547249 | 0.010264 |
| SVD-Gap | **1.19 × 10⁻⁷** | **0.000977** | **0.000846** | 0.007606 |
| SVD-Tangent | 0.067624 | 0.202853 | 0.201175 | **0.003023** |
| SVD-Gap-Budget | 0.068559 | 0.228668 | 0.225554 | 0.007626 |
| SVD-Subspace | **1.19 × 10⁻⁷** | 0.001016 | **0.000846** | 0.010260 |

The strongest spectral preservation was achieved by SVD-Gap and SVD-Subspace.

Both methods reduced the maximum recorded relative change in selected leading singular values to approximately numerical precision.

In contrast, the unrestricted PGD baseline showed a mean maximum recorded relative singular-value error of approximately 0.083.

The SVD-Gap-Budget ablation had a comparable perturbation magnitude to SVD-Gap, but its singular-value and subspace errors were much larger.

This comparison strongly supports the conclusion that **the projection direction, rather than the adaptive budget alone, is responsible for exact preservation of the selected dominant singular structure**.

### 8.2 Preserving Dominant Structure Versus Reducing Overall Spectral Drift

An important distinction emerged between dominant-structure preservation and overall spectral change.

SVD-Subspace preserved the selected top-k structure almost exactly, despite producing approximately the same mean overall spectral drift as conventional PGD.

By comparison, SVD-Tangent had the lowest overall spectral drift but did not preserve the leading singular values exactly.

This distinction is expected because the two metrics measure different properties:

- **Top-k singular-value and subspace errors** measure changes in the selected dominant singular structure.
- **Spectral drift** measures relative changes across the singular spectrum used by the diagnostic.

Therefore, a perturbation can strongly preserve selected dominant components while still changing the rest of the spectrum.

Conversely, a small overall spectral change does not imply exact preservation of the dominant singular components.

### 8.3 Perturbation Magnitude Relative to the Spectral Gap

| Method | Maximum recorded ‖δ‖ / spectral gap |
|---|---:|
| PGD | 4.3048 |
| SVD-Gap | 0.9000 |
| SVD-Tangent | 0.4500 |
| SVD-Gap-Budget | 0.9000 |
| SVD-Subspace | 4.3048 |

The recorded ratios are consistent with the implemented adaptive budgets.

For SVD-Gap, the perturbation-to-gap ratio remained at or below approximately 0.9.

For SVD-Tangent, it remained at or below approximately 0.45.

SVD-Subspace exceeded the spectral-gap threshold in some cases while still preserving the leading singular structure to numerical precision in the sampled diagnostics.

This observation illustrates that the gap budget provides a sufficient protection condition, but that the same structure may remain preserved for some perturbations exceeding that budget.

### 8.4 Interpretation of the Collapse Diagnostic

The experimental logs reported `collapse_fraction = 0` for all methods.

However, the current diagnostic computes the post-attack gap from singular values already sorted in descending order.

Since consecutive sorted singular values cannot have a negative gap, this quantity cannot reliably detect changes in the *identity* of the dominant singular directions.

Therefore, the observed zero collapse fraction should **not** be interpreted as proof that no singular modes exchanged their structural roles.

The singular-value errors and left/right subspace errors provide more informative evidence of structural preservation.

A revised mode-crossing diagnostic would improve the reliability of future experiments.

## 9. Ablation Study: Separating Geometry and Perturbation Magnitude

The SVD-Gap, SVD-Gap-Budget, and SVD-Subspace configurations allow several important comparisons.

### 9.1 SVD-Gap Versus SVD-Gap-Budget

Both methods used nearly identical mean perturbation budgets:

- SVD-Gap: ε ≈ 2.2342
- SVD-Gap-Budget: ε ≈ 2.2344

However, their spectral-preservation results differed substantially.

SVD-Gap reduced the selected singular-value error to approximately 1.19 × 10⁻⁷.

SVD-Gap-Budget showed an error of approximately 0.0686.

This supports the effectiveness of the complementary-subspace projection in preserving dominant singular structure.

Interestingly, SVD-Gap-Budget achieved a higher average clean decoding R²:

- SVD-Gap: 0.6846
- SVD-Gap-Budget: 0.6903

Thus, exact dominant spectral preservation did not automatically lead to better clean decoding.

### 9.2 SVD-Gap Versus SVD-Subspace

Both methods used complementary-subspace projection.

The main difference was perturbation magnitude:

- SVD-Gap: mean ε ≈ 2.2342
- SVD-Subspace: fixed ε = 3

Both maintained the protected singular structure to numerical precision.

SVD-Subspace achieved:

- Higher mean clean decoding R².
- Higher R² under SVD-Gap evaluation attacks.
- Higher mean Poisson-corruption R².
- Lower R² under 30% neuron dropout.

This comparison indicates that a smaller spectral-gap-based budget is not universally beneficial once the complementary-subspace projection already provides strong structural preservation.

### 9.3 SVD-Tangent Versus Complementary-Subspace Methods

SVD-Tangent explores a different perturbation geometry.

Rather than explicitly eliminating changes to the dominant singular directions, it permits structured changes in directions associated with the tangent space of the rank-\(k\) matrix manifold.

Its empirical behavior was markedly different:

- Higher average clean decoding R².
- Strongly improved resistance to severe neuron dropout.
- Much lower unrestricted-PGD robustness.
- Lower Poisson-corruption robustness.
- Greater changes in selected dominant singular directions.

These results suggest that perturbation geometry may influence the specific forms of invariance learned by the neural encoder.

However, establishing a causal relationship between tangent-space perturbations and robustness to neuron loss requires additional controls, particularly matched-budget PGD training and evaluation across more recording sessions.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 Main Scientific Finding: Dominant Spectral Structure Can Be Preserved During Attack Generation

The clearest result of this experiment is the accurate preservation of the selected dominant singular values and subspaces under complementary-subspace-constrained attacks.

SVD-Gap and SVD-Subspace both achieved relative top-k singular-value errors near 10⁻⁷.

The gap-budget-only ablation did not achieve comparable preservation, despite using a similar average perturbation magnitude.

This supports the central mathematical and algorithmic contribution of restricting adversarial perturbations to carefully selected spectral directions.

### 11.2 Improvement in Clean Decoding Is Promising but Modest

All four spectral methods achieved a higher *mean* clean decoding R² than conventional PGD.

The improvements ranged from approximately +0.0035 for SVD-Gap to +0.0109 for SVD-Tangent.

SVD-Subspace showed a particularly consistent advantage across all three seeds, with a mean improvement of +0.0097 R².

Nevertheless, only three seeds and one recording session were evaluated.

The differences should therefore be interpreted as preliminary descriptive evidence rather than statistically established performance gains.

### 11.3 Robustness Is Strongly Dependent on the Perturbation Type

The results do not support a general claim that spectral adversarial training improves robustness against arbitrary attacks.

Conventional PGD training remained strongest under unrestricted PGD evaluation.

However, SVD-Tangent achieved a substantial improvement in simulated severe neural unit loss.

This suggests that **robustness to adversarial perturbations and robustness to recording degradation are distinct properties**.

A model trained with one restricted adversarial geometry may develop invariances that benefit a particular type of recording disturbance without improving resistance to unrelated adversarial directions.

This trade-off warrants further investigation.

### 11.4 The SVD-Tangent Result Suggests a Research Direction

SVD-Tangent increased mean R² under 30% neuron dropout by approximately +0.1005 compared with PGD, consistently across all three seeds.

This effect was considerably larger than the observed clean decoding improvements.

A possible interpretation is that tangent-space-constrained adversarial training changes how distributed neural information is represented, making the decoder less sensitive to the loss of a subset of neural units.

However, this remains a hypothesis.

The current experiments do not directly measure representational redundancy, neuron-level attribution, or manifold stability after neuron loss.

These measurements would be necessary to support a mechanistic interpretation.

### 11.5 Spectral Preservation Does Not Guarantee Better Downstream Performance

An important negative result is that exact preservation of selected singular components did not consistently produce superior decoding or robustness.

For example, SVD-Gap preserved the dominant singular structure more accurately than SVD-Gap-Budget, but the latter achieved higher mean clean decoding R².

Similarly, SVD-Subspace maintained exact spectral preservation but performed worse than PGD under severe neuron dropout.

These findings indicate that the mathematical property of spectral preservation and the downstream utility of an adversarial training strategy must be evaluated independently.

## 12. Limitations and Next Experiments

Several limitations should be addressed before drawing stronger scientific conclusions.

**Limited number of recording sessions.** The experiment used only C-CO12. Additional sessions are necessary to assess generalization.

**Small number of random seeds.** Three seeds provide an initial estimate of variability but are insufficient for strong statistical claims.

**Missing non-adversarial CEBRA baseline.** A clean-trained encoder should be evaluated to distinguish improvements over ordinary CEBRA from improvements over PGD adversarial training.

**Different effective perturbation budgets.** The SVD-based methods use different effective budgets despite sharing the same maximum epsilon. Matched-budget PGD controls are required.

**Restricted robustness evaluation.** Robustness was measured using a specified 20-step PGD protocol and two constrained attack variants. Additional attack configurations would improve the evaluation.

**Simplified recording corruptions.** The neuron-dropout experiment simulates loss of units by setting selected channels to zero. It does not directly evaluate longitudinal electrode drift or empirically observed neuron disappearance.

**Input validity.** Perturbations are generated in a continuous numerical space, and the present evaluation does not establish that all adversarial samples remain valid integer, nonnegative neural spike counts.

**Spectral metric limitations.** The current collapse diagnostic does not reliably detect all dominant-mode exchanges and should be revised.

**Reproducibility across runs.** A previous single-seed pilot produced somewhat different R² values from the current experiment for some shared configurations. The present 15-run experiment is treated as the primary result set, but bitwise reproducibility across environments has not been established.

### Recommended Follow-Up Experiments

The immediate priorities are:

1. **Matched-budget PGD controls:** Repeat conventional PGD training using ε ≈ 2.23 and ε ≈ 1.18 to evaluate the contribution of effective perturbation strength.
2. **Clean CEBRA baseline:** Train and evaluate the same encoder and decoder without adversarial perturbations.
3. **Additional neural sessions:** Repeat the comparison across multiple recordings or animals.
4. **Further robustness testing:** Evaluate larger neuron-loss fractions, structured or behaviorally selective unit loss, and multiple corruption severities.
5. **Improved spectral diagnostics:** Directly track dominant singular-subspace identity and spectral mode exchanges.
6. **Mechanistic analysis:** Investigate whether tangent-space training increases redundancy or changes the distribution of behavioral information across neural features.
7. **Extended statistical evaluation:** Use more independent training seeds and appropriate paired comparisons across sessions.

## 13. Conclusion

This study evaluated five adversarial training strategies for CEBRA-based neural representation learning using 15 completed experiments on the C-CO12 dataset.

The experimental results support three main conclusions.

**First, spectral geometry provides an effective mechanism for controlling adversarial perturbations.** Complementary-subspace-constrained attacks preserved the selected dominant singular structure to approximately numerical precision, with modest additional computational cost.

**Second, spectral adversarial training can modestly improve clean behavioral decoding.** SVD-Tangent achieved the highest average R², while SVD-Subspace provided the most consistent improvement over conventional PGD across the three seeds.

**Third, the type of adversarial geometry strongly influences the type of robustness obtained.** SVD-Tangent substantially improved resistance to the simulated loss of 30% of recorded neurons, but this benefit was accompanied by severe vulnerability to unrestricted PGD attacks.

These findings suggest that spectral constraints should not be viewed simply as a mechanism for increasing adversarial robustness. Rather, they define different perturbation geometries that can influence representation quality, spectral preservation, and sensitivity to distinct forms of recording degradation.

The strongest current results are the precise preservation of selected singular components and the consistent improvement of SVD-Tangent under severe simulated neuron dropout.

Further matched-budget experiments and replication across additional neural recordings are needed to determine whether these observations represent generalizable properties of spectral-geometry-constrained neural representation learning.
