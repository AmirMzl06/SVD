# Acorn-SVD: Experimental Results on the C-CO12 Neural Recording Dataset

## 1. Experimental Overview

This report presents the experimental results obtained using the **Acorn-SVD** fork of CEBRA, evaluating conventional Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) against two SVD-based adversarial training strategies.

The primary objective was to investigate whether adversarial perturbations constrained by the dominant singular structure of neural population activity could improve behavioral decoding performance and robustness while preserving important spectral properties of the input data.

Three adversarial training configurations were evaluated:

1. **PGD (`pgd`)** — Standard L2-constrained PGD without spectral restrictions.
2. **SVD-Gap (`svd_gap`)** — A spectrally constrained attack combining subspace projection with an adaptive perturbation budget determined by the singular-value gap.
3. **SVD-Subspace (`svd_subspace`)** — An ablation using subspace-projected perturbations with a fixed L2 budget, without the spectral-gap-dependent budget restriction.

All three models were trained and evaluated successfully using the same neural recording session, random seed, encoder architecture, decoder architecture, and training configuration.

**Dataset:** C-CO12  
**Random seed:** 42  
**Number of models:** 3  
**Encoder training iterations:** 3,000 per model  
**Maximum adversarial perturbation budget:** ε = 3.0  
**GPU environment:** NVIDIA H200, RunAI

### 2.3 SVD-Based Perturbation Construction

For a centered neural activity window with singular value decomposition

\[
X_c=U\Sigma V^\top,
\]

the SVD-based attacks constrain the perturbation direction using projections onto the orthogonal complements of the dominant left and right singular subspaces:

\[
G_{\perp}=(I-U_kU_k^\top)G(I-V_kV_k^\top).
\]

For SVD-Gap, the permitted perturbation magnitude is also adjusted according to the singular-value gap:

\[
\epsilon_i=\min\left(3,\;0.9(\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1})\right).
\]

This produces a window-dependent perturbation budget while restricting perturbations to directions outside the protected dominant singular subspaces.

The SVD-Subspace ablation applies the subspace restriction but retains the fixed perturbation budget of ε = 3.

## 3. Main Behavioral Decoding Results

### 3.1 Overall Validation R²

The primary decoding metric was the average coefficient of determination (R²) across all six behavioral labels.

| Training method | Validation R² (clean) | R² under PGD attack | R² under SVD-Gap attack |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **PGD** | 0.685451 | **0.563765** | 0.625275 |
| **SVD-Gap** | **0.691389** | 0.537288 | **0.626628** |
| **SVD-Subspace** | 0.676926 | 0.555322 | 0.626024 |

**SVD-Gap achieved the highest clean validation R²**, reaching 0.691389 compared with 0.685451 for conventional PGD.

The absolute improvement was:

\[
\Delta R^2_{\text{clean}}
=0.691389-0.685451
=+0.005938.
\]

However, conventional PGD adversarial training achieved the highest R² under the unrestricted PGD evaluation attack.

Under this attack, SVD-Gap reached 0.537288, compared with 0.563765 for the PGD-trained model:

\[
\Delta R^2_{\text{PGD attack}}
=0.537288-0.563765
=-0.026477.
\]

Under the SVD-Gap evaluation attack, SVD-Gap training produced the highest score, although the advantage was small:

\[
\Delta R^2_{\text{SVD-Gap attack}}
=0.626628-0.625275
=+0.001353.
\]

SVD-Subspace training performed below the PGD baseline on clean validation data and under the unrestricted PGD evaluation attack.

These results indicate that spectral constraints can improve clean decoding in this experiment without necessarily improving robustness against unrestricted adversarial perturbations.

The attacks optimized the prediction Mean Squared Error using the validation targets.

The evaluation settings were:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Attack norm | L2 |
| Maximum ε | 3.0 |
| Evaluation attack steps | 20 |
| Evaluation step size | 0.375 |

| Evaluation mode | Frozen encoder and decoder |

- **Unrestricted PGD:** No spectral constraint.
- **SVD-Gap PGD:** Subspace projection combined with the adaptive spectral-gap budget.

The evaluation used 4,508 valid neural windows after accounting for the temporal offset requirements of the encoder.

### 5.1 Perturbation Magnitudes and Adaptive Budgets

| Training statistic | PGD | SVD-Gap | SVD-Subspace |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mean adversarial training loss | 6.508900 | 6.435915 | 6.478465 |
| Mean perturbation L2 norm | 2.996329 | 2.232160 | 2.997053 |
| Mean allowed ε | — | 2.234126 | 3.000000 |
| Mean logged minimum ε | — | 0.738703 | 3.000000 |
| Fraction of gap-limited windows | — | 0.802810 | 0.000000 |
| Mean protected rank k | — | 1.438029 | 1.437928 |
| Mean singular-value gap | — | 2.627702 | 2.628475 |

The mean perturbation norm for conventional PGD was approximately 2.996, showing that the attack utilized nearly the entire permitted L2 budget.

SVD-Subspace behaved similarly in terms of perturbation magnitude, with a mean norm of approximately 2.997.

In contrast, SVD-Gap produced a mean perturbation norm of approximately 2.232, closely matching its mean adaptive budget of 2.234.

The fraction of gap-limited windows was:

\[
0.802810 \times 100 = 80.281\%.
\]

Thus, the spectral-gap constraint reduced the permitted perturbation magnitude in approximately **80.3% of training windows**.

The average protected rank was approximately 1.44 for both SVD-based approaches, indicating that the algorithm typically selected a small number of dominant singular directions.

This suggests that the method primarily preserved the leading one or two singular components rather than a high-dimensional dominant subspace.

### 5.2 Spectral Preservation Diagnostics

The following diagnostics were recorded during training.

| Spectral diagnostic | PGD | SVD-Gap | SVD-Subspace |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Maximum singular-value error | 0.0912249 | **1.1912 × 10⁻⁷** | **1.1912 × 10⁻⁷** |
| Maximum left-subspace error | 0.477539 | **0.000976563** | **0.000976563** |
| Maximum right-subspace error | 0.472576 | **0.000845728** | **0.000845728** |
| Mean spectral drift | 0.0102472 | **0.00757419** | 0.0102662 |
| Maximum perturbation-to-gap ratio | 4.304829 | **0.900000** | 4.304830 |

The most striking result is the difference in singular-value preservation.

Conventional PGD produced a maximum recorded singular-value error of approximately 0.0912.

Both SVD-based methods reduced this diagnostic to approximately:

\[
1.19\times10^{-7}.
\]

This is consistent with preservation of the selected dominant singular values to numerical precision in the sampled diagnostic evaluations.

The dominant left and right singular-subspace errors were also substantially lower for the SVD-based attacks.

For SVD-Gap training, the maximum perturbation-to-gap ratio was approximately 0.9, consistent with the configured γ = 0.9.

For SVD-Subspace training, this ratio reached approximately 4.30, because no gap-dependent magnitude restriction was applied.

Importantly, SVD-Subspace still maintained very small errors in the protected singular values and subspaces.

This suggests that the subspace projection itself is the central mechanism responsible for preserving the selected dominant singular structure, while the spectral-gap budget provides an additional restriction on perturbation magnitude.

### 5.3 Spectral Drift Comparison

The recorded mean spectral-drift values were:

- PGD: 0.0102472
- SVD-Gap: 0.00757419
- SVD-Subspace: 0.0102662

Relative to PGD, the SVD-Gap method reduced the mean spectral-drift diagnostic by approximately **26.1%**.

However, SVD-Subspace exhibited nearly the same mean spectral drift as conventional PGD.

This indicates that preserving selected dominant singular components does not necessarily minimize the overall spectral-drift diagnostic.

The gap-dependent budget appears to provide additional control over the magnitude of spectral changes.

These spectral-preservation findings concern the selected, centered spectral structure and the metrics recorded by the implementation. They should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every singular value, feature mean, or physiological property of the input remains unchanged.

## 8. Interpretation and Discussion

The experiments reveal three important findings.

### 8.1 Spectral Preservation Was Successfully Demonstrated

The strongest result is the substantial reduction in dominant singular-value and subspace errors under both SVD-based attacks.

The maximum singular-value error fell from approximately 0.0912 for conventional PGD to approximately \(1.19\times10^{-7}\) for both SVD-Gap and SVD-Subspace.

This supports the effectiveness of the proposed subspace-projection mechanism for preserving selected dominant spectral components of the neural input windows.

The SVD-Subspace ablation is particularly informative because it achieved nearly identical preservation diagnostics without enforcing the spectral-gap-dependent perturbation budget.

Consequently, the current results suggest that subspace projection is primarily responsible for the preservation of the selected dominant singular structure.

### 8.2 Spectral-Gap Constraints Alter Attack Strength

The SVD-Gap method imposed a smaller effective perturbation budget for approximately 80.3% of training windows.

Its mean allowed ε was 2.234, compared with the fixed ε = 3 used by conventional PGD and SVD-Subspace.

This difference must be considered when interpreting downstream performance.

Improved clean decoding under SVD-Gap training may be associated with the direction of the adversarial perturbations, their smaller magnitude, or a combination of both.

The current three-arm experiment does not completely isolate these effects.

An additional **SVD-Gap-Budget ablation**, which applies the gap-dependent budget without subspace projection, would help determine the separate contribution of adaptive perturbation magnitude.

### 8.3 Clean Decoding Improved, but General Robustness Did Not

SVD-Gap training achieved the highest clean validation R² of 0.691389, compared with 0.685451 for PGD and 0.676926 for SVD-Subspace.

However, SVD-Gap training did not improve robustness against unrestricted PGD.

In fact, the SVD-Gap-trained model achieved a lower R² under PGD attack than the conventional PGD-trained model.

This indicates a distinction between **spectral preservation during adversarial training** and **general adversarial robustness after training**.

A method can successfully preserve the dominant spectral structure of its training perturbations without necessarily producing an encoder that is more robust to unconstrained adversarial attacks.

The results under SVD-Gap evaluation were very similar across the three models, with all mean R² scores near 0.626.

Therefore, the present evidence supports spectral preservation more strongly than improvements in adversarial robustness.

## 9. Limitations

The present experiment has several limitations relevant to scientific interpretation.

**Single random seed.** All experiments used Seed 42. Additional seeds are required to estimate variability and determine whether the relatively small clean R² improvements are reproducible.

**Single recording session.** Only C-CO12 was evaluated. Generalization to other neural recording sessions remains untested.

**Unequal effective attack budgets.** Although the maximum ε was 3 in all methods, the SVD-Gap training attack had a lower mean effective budget.

**Incomplete ablation coverage.** The gap-budget-only ablation was not included in the reported run, making it difficult to isolate the effects of subspace projection and adaptive budget control.

**Restricted spectral preservation objective.** The SVD-based methods target selected dominant singular components, not necessarily the entire singular spectrum.

**Input-domain interpretation.** The recorded configuration used centered spectral computations without explicit mean preservation. The present report does not establish that all generated perturbations correspond to physically valid integer, nonnegative spike-count modifications.

**Robustness evaluation scope.** The reported attacks provide an informative comparison under the specified 20-step evaluation protocol, but they do not establish worst-case robustness across all possible attacks.

## 10. Conclusion

The Acorn-SVD experiments successfully evaluated conventional PGD, SVD-Gap, and SVD-Subspace adversarial training on the C-CO12 neural recording session.

The main findings are:

1. **SVD-Gap produced the highest clean behavioral decoding performance**, reaching a mean validation R² of **0.691389**, compared with **0.685451** for PGD.

2. **Both SVD-based methods strongly preserved the selected dominant singular structure**, reducing the maximum recorded singular-value error to approximately **1.19 × 10⁻⁷**.

3. **SVD-Gap reduced the mean effective perturbation budget to approximately 2.234**, with the spectral-gap constraint active for **80.3%** of training windows.

4. **Conventional PGD remained the strongest model against unrestricted PGD evaluation**, achieving R² = **0.563765**, compared with **0.537288** for SVD-Gap.

5. **Performance under SVD-Gap evaluation was nearly identical across the three models**, with mean R² values between **0.625275** and **0.626628**.

6. **The additional computational overhead was moderate**, with SVD-Gap requiring approximately **11.52 minutes** of encoder training versus **10.02 minutes** for PGD.

Overall, these findings provide strong preliminary empirical support for the **spectral-preservation properties of SVD-constrained adversarial perturbations**.

They also demonstrate that preserving dominant singular structures does not automatically translate into stronger robustness against unrestricted PGD attacks.

The most promising result is the combination of highly accurate spectral preservation and a modest improvement in clean behavioral decoding under SVD-Gap training.

Further experiments with multiple seeds, additional neural sessions, and the gap-budget-only ablation are needed to establish whether these effects are reproducible and to identify the distinct contributions of spectral projection and adaptive perturbation budgets.
