**Experiment date:** October 17, 2026  
**Dataset:** C-CO12 Neural Recording Session

**Computational environment:** EPFL RunAI, NVIDIA H200 GPU

# Abstract

This report presents an expanded experimental evaluation of **Acorn-SVD**, a framework for adversarial training of neural representation models using perturbations constrained by the singular geometry of neural activity.

The framework extends conventional projected gradient descent (PGD) by constructing adversarial perturbations relative to the dominant singular subspaces of temporally structured neural activity. The objective is to investigate whether perturbation geometry can influence the spectral properties, behavioral decoding accuracy, and robustness of learned neural representations.

Nine training strategies were evaluated: standard non-adversarial CEBRA, conventional PGD, SVD-Gap, SVD-Gap-Uncapped, SVD-Tangent, SVD-Split, SVD-Tangent-Budget, SVD-Subspace, and SVD-Gap-Budget.

The experiments used the C-CO12 neural recording dataset, containing 86 neural input features and six behavioral outputs. Every strategy was trained with three random seeds (42, 43, and 44), resulting in 27 completed experiments.

Each encoder was trained for 3,000 iterations using the same CEBRA architecture. A separate two-layer MLP decoder was subsequently trained for 2,500 epochs. Evaluation included clean behavioral decoding, three gradient-based adversarial attacks, and simulated neural recording corruptions: Poisson resampling, 10% and 30% neuron dropout, and multiplicative gain drift.

The principal findings are:

1. **SVD-Subspace achieved the highest clean validation performance**, with mean R² = **0.6952 ± 0.0038**, compared with **0.6775 ± 0.0089** for conventional PGD and **0.6841 ± 0.0038** for non-adversarial CEBRA.

2. **SVD-Subspace also achieved the highest mean R² under unrestricted PGD evaluation**, reaching **0.5708 ± 0.0110**, slightly above the PGD-trained baseline of **0.5668 ± 0.0110**. This small difference is not consistent across all seeds and should not yet be regarded as statistically established.

3. **SVD-Gap, SVD-Gap-Uncapped, and SVD-Subspace preserved selected dominant singular values to approximately numerical precision**, with maximum recorded relative singular-value errors near \(1.2\times10^{-7}\).

4. **SVD-Tangent achieved the highest performance under 30% neuron dropout among adversarially trained models**, with R² = **0.5291 ± 0.0058**, compared with **0.4345 ± 0.0244** for PGD.

5. However, **non-adversarial CEBRA achieved a nearly identical neuron-dropout score**, R² = **0.5268 ± 0.0119**. This changes the interpretation of the earlier result: SVD-Tangent appears to preserve a level of neuron-dropout tolerance that conventional PGD training substantially reduces, rather than clearly outperforming the clean baseline.

6. **SVD-Tangent remained highly vulnerable to unrestricted PGD**, achieving only **0.1972 ± 0.0293**. The matched-budget ablation, SVD-Tangent-Budget, performed considerably better under PGD but worse under neuron dropout, supporting the hypothesis that perturbation direction influences the observed robustness trade-off.

7. **SVD-Split provided an intermediate compromise**, achieving R² = **0.5549 ± 0.0041** under unrestricted PGD and **0.4573 ± 0.0186** under 30% neuron dropout. However, it did not simultaneously match the strongest PGD robustness and the strongest neuron-dropout robustness.

These results suggest that the geometry of adversarial perturbations influences the forms of robustness learned by the neural encoder. At the same time, they show that precise spectral preservation, strong unrestricted adversarial robustness, and tolerance to missing neural units are distinct objectives that do not necessarily improve together.

# 1. Research Motivation and Objectives

CEBRA learns low-dimensional representations of neural population activity using contrastive learning. Such representations can encode behaviorally relevant information and provide a framework for studying neural population dynamics.

In practical applications, however, neural recordings may be affected by several types of perturbations:

- Changes in neuronal firing activity.
- Stochastic spike-count fluctuations.
- Loss of recorded neural units.
- Changes in the gain or sensitivity of individual recording channels.
- Adversarial perturbations designed to degrade downstream predictions.

Conventional PGD adversarial training improves resistance to norm-bounded perturbations by exposing the model to inputs that increase its training loss.

However, ordinary PGD does not explicitly account for the structure of neural population activity.

The Acorn-SVD project investigates an alternative approach in which adversarial perturbations are defined using the singular value decomposition of neural activity windows.

Rather than allowing arbitrary perturbations within a fixed L2 budget, the method controls the directions and, in some cases, magnitudes of perturbations relative to dominant singular subspaces.

The expanded study addresses four questions.

**RQ1 — Spectral preservation**

Can adversarial perturbations preserve selected dominant singular values and singular subspaces while still modifying neural activity?

**RQ2 — Behavioral decoding**

Does spectral-geometry-constrained adversarial training improve downstream behavioral decoding compared with both conventional PGD and non-adversarial CEBRA?

**RQ3 — Robustness specialization**

Do different spectral perturbation geometries produce different forms of robustness, particularly under adversarial attacks and simulated loss of recorded neurons?

**RQ4 — Combining geometric constraints**

Can a method that combines complementary-subspace and tangent-space perturbations provide a more favorable trade-off between adversarial robustness and robustness to recording corruption?

The newly introduced **SVD-Split** method was specifically evaluated in relation to the fourth question.

# 2. Mathematical Formulation

## 2.1 Neural Activity and Singular Value Decomposition

Let a neural activity window be represented as:

\[
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T},
\]

where \(N\) is the number of neural features and \(T\) is the temporal window length.

The input matrix is temporally centered:

\[
X_c=XC,
\]

where \(C\) is the temporal centering operator.

The singular value decomposition is then:

\[
X_c=U\Sigma V^\top.
\]

The singular values are ordered as:

\[
\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq 0.
\]

For a selected rank \(k\), the spectral gap is:

\[
g_k=\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1}.
\]

The algorithm uses an adaptive protected rank within the configured range:

\[
1\leq k\leq 8.
\]

The protected rank and spectral gap depend on the structure of the individual neural activity window.

## 2.2 Complementary-Subspace Perturbations

Let \(G\) denote the adversarial gradient.

The projection onto the orthogonal complement of the dominant left and right singular subspaces is:

\[
P_S(G)
=
(I-U_kU_k^\top)G(I-V_kV_k^\top).
\]

Perturbations generated within this space avoid directly modifying the protected dominant singular directions.

Under an appropriate spectral separation condition, this construction can preserve the selected leading singular values and subspaces.

The **SVD-Subspace** method uses this projection with a fixed perturbation budget.

The **SVD-Gap** method combines the projection with an adaptive budget.

## 2.3 Spectral-Gap-Adaptive Budget

For SVD-Gap, the perturbation budget of window \(i\) is:

\[
\epsilon_i
=
\min\left(
3,\gamma g_k
\right),
\]

with:

\[
\gamma=0.9.
\]

Consequently, different windows may receive different maximum perturbation magnitudes.

This introduces an additional constraint that depends on the spectral geometry of the recording.

## 2.4 Tangent-Space Perturbations

The complementary tangent-space projection is:

\[
P_T(G)=G-P_S(G).
\]

Unlike complementary-subspace perturbations, tangent-space perturbations can affect the dominant singular structure.

The SVD-Tangent method uses the budget:

\[
\epsilon_{T,i}
=
\min\left(
3,\frac{\gamma g_k}{2}
\right).
\]

With \(\gamma=0.9\):

\[
\epsilon_{T,i}=\min(3,0.45g_k).
\]

The reduced budget provides a sufficient bound for controlling changes in the selected spectral gap under the assumptions of the method.

However, preserving a positive spectral gap is not equivalent to preserving the original dominant singular values exactly.

## 2.5 SVD-Split: Combining Complement and Tangent Perturbations

The expanded fork introduces **SVD-Split**.

The perturbation is decomposed into complementary and tangent components:

\[
\boxed{\Delta=\Delta_S+\Delta_T}
\]

where:

\[
\Delta_S\in S_k,\qquad \Delta_T\in T_k.
\]

The two components are optimized using separate budgets.

The final attack is then certified using operator-norm constraints designed to satisfy:

\[
\boxed{
\|\Delta_S\|_2+2\|\Delta_T\|_2<g_k
}
\]

This is a sufficient condition for maintaining a positive selected spectral gap.

Unlike SVD-Subspace, SVD-Split does not guarantee that the original leading singular values remain unchanged.

Its objective is instead to allow both forms of perturbation while controlling the risk of spectral-gap closure.

An important experimental qualification is that the two components have separate Frobenius-norm budgets. The recorded value `adv_epsilon=3` does not, by itself, establish that the L2 norm of the final combined perturbation is at most 3 for every window.

The observed average total perturbation norm is reported separately below.

# 3. Experimental Methods

## 3.1 Evaluated Training Strategies

Nine methods were evaluated.

| Method | Training mode | Perturbation geometry | Budget mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clean | Standard CEBRA | No adversarial perturbation | None |
| PGD | Adversarial | Unrestricted | Fixed ε = 3 |
| SVD-Gap | Adversarial | Complementary subspace | min(3, 0.9 × gap) |
| SVD-Gap-Uncapped | Adversarial | Complementary subspace | 0.9 × gap, without the ε=3 cap |
| SVD-Tangent | Adversarial | Tangent space | min(3, 0.45 × gap) |
| SVD-Split | Adversarial | Complement + tangent | Separate budgets and joint spectral certificate |
| SVD-Tangent-Budget | Adversarial | Unrestricted | min(3, 0.45 × gap) |
| SVD-Subspace | Adversarial | Complementary subspace | Fixed ε = 3 |
| SVD-Gap-Budget | Adversarial | Unrestricted | min(3, 0.9 × gap) |

The last two budget-only methods help separate the influence of perturbation geometry from that of perturbation magnitude.

The SVD-Gap-Uncapped arm serves as an additional test of the spectral-gap budget without the ordinary ε=3 cap.

For this arm, the configured nominal epsilon was 1000 and the PGD step size was 200. Its actual perturbation magnitude nevertheless remained controlled by the spectral-gap constraint.

## 3.5 Reproducibility and Seeds

Three independent training seeds were used:

\[
\{42,43,44\}.
\]

The revised fork also changes the neural batch sampler's default seeding behavior so that it derives its random seed from the initialized PyTorch random seed rather than uncontrolled system entropy.

This is an improvement for repeatability and controlled comparison.

Nevertheless, identical seed labels alone do not establish bitwise reproducibility of every GPU computation or identical minibatch sequences across different solver configurations.

No such stronger guarantee is assumed in this report.

Each trained encoder–decoder pair was evaluated using three attacks against the frozen prediction pipeline.

| Evaluation parameter | Value |

3. SVD-Tangent-constrained PGD.

However, the effective permitted budgets differ by attack family.

| Evaluation attack | Mean permitted budget |
|---|---:|

## 3.7 Neural Recording Corruption Protocol

The evaluation included four simulated forms of neural recording degradation.

| Corruption | Description |
|---|---|
| Poisson resampling | Resample each neural count using a Poisson distribution with rate determined by the nonnegative input |
| 10% neuron dropout | Zero approximately 9 of 86 randomly selected neural channels |
| 30% neuron dropout | Zero approximately 26 of 86 randomly selected neural channels |
| Gain drift | Apply independent multiplicative gains to neural channels |

The gain factors were generated as:

\[
g_n=\exp(z_n),\quad z_n\sim\mathcal N(0,0.3^2).
\]

Each corruption condition was evaluated using five independently generated random draws, with the same evaluation random seed.

The corrupted validation recordings were processed using the frozen encoder and decoder. No retraining was performed.

## 5.2 Severe Neuron Dropout: The Clean Baseline Changes the Interpretation

The mean R² under 30% neuron dropout was:

| Method | Mean R² |
|---|---:|
| Clean CEBRA | 0.5268 |
| PGD | 0.4345 |
| SVD-Tangent | **0.5291** |
| SVD-Split | 0.4573 |
| SVD-Tangent-Budget | 0.4758 |
| SVD-Subspace | 0.4125 |

Relative to conventional PGD, SVD-Tangent improved mean R² by:

\[
0.52905-0.43448=+0.09458.
\]

This is a relative improvement of approximately 21.8% in R².

However, relative to Clean CEBRA:

\[
0.52905-0.52676=+0.00229.
\]

Therefore, **SVD-Tangent did not demonstrate a meaningful average advantage over non-adversarial CEBRA under 30% neuron dropout in this experiment**.

A more defensible interpretation is that conventional PGD adversarial training substantially reduced tolerance to the removal of recorded neurons, whereas tangent-space training retained approximately the dropout tolerance observed in the clean-trained encoder.

This does not make the SVD-Tangent result unimportant.

Rather, it suggests that different adversarial training geometries have different effects on the natural recording-corruption robustness of the learned representation.

This supports the interpretation that tangent-space-constrained training retains a different sensitivity profile to missing neural channels compared with complementary-subspace adversarial training.

# 7. Ablation Analysis

## 7.1 Tangent Geometry Versus Tangent Budget

The comparison between SVD-Tangent and SVD-Tangent-Budget is particularly informative.

Both methods use approximately the same adaptive budget.

Their main difference is the perturbation direction.

| Metric | SVD-Tangent | SVD-Tangent-Budget | Difference (Tangent − Budget) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Clean R² | 0.6913 | 0.6905 | +0.0008 |
| Unrestricted PGD R² | 0.1972 | 0.5114 | −0.3143 |
| SVD-Gap attack R² | 0.4514 | 0.6047 | −0.1533 |
| 30% neuron dropout R² | 0.5291 | 0.4758 | +0.0532 |
| Poisson R² | 0.6244 | 0.6629 | −0.0385 |

The two methods achieved almost identical average clean decoding performance.

However, they exhibited very different robustness profiles.

SVD-Tangent achieved higher neuron-dropout robustness but substantially lower robustness against PGD and Poisson corruption.

Since their permitted budget distributions are closely matched, this supports the hypothesis that **perturbation geometry contributes to the robustness trade-off**.

The comparison is stronger than a direct comparison with conventional PGD, because it better controls for perturbation magnitude.

However, this still does not prove a complete causal explanation for the observed differences.

## 7.2 SVD-Gap Versus Gap-Budget-Only

The SVD-Gap and SVD-Gap-Budget methods also use approximately the same adaptive magnitude restrictions.

Their main difference is whether complementary-subspace projection is used.

| Metric | SVD-Gap | SVD-Gap-Budget |
|---|---:|---:|
| Clean R² | 0.6863 | 0.6840 |
| Unrestricted PGD R² | 0.5358 | 0.5445 |
| SVD-Gap attack R² | 0.6221 | 0.6166 |
| 30% neuron dropout R² | 0.4270 | 0.4460 |
| Mean effective ε | 2.2340 | 2.2340 |
| Maximum recorded top-k singular-value error | ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ | ≈ 0.0684 |

The complementary-subspace projection dramatically reduced changes in the protected singular structure.

However, the improvements in downstream behavioral performance were smaller and inconsistent across metrics.

This reinforces the distinction between **structural preservation** and **downstream utility**.

## 7.3 SVD-Gap Versus SVD-Subspace

Both strategies restrict perturbations to the complementary subspace.

The primary difference is budget magnitude:

- SVD-Gap: mean permitted budget approximately 2.234.
- SVD-Subspace: fixed budget of 3.0.

Both achieved numerical-precision preservation of selected dominant singular values.

However, SVD-Subspace achieved better average clean decoding and stronger performance under several evaluation conditions.

This indicates that the adaptive spectral-gap budget is not necessarily beneficial once the complementary-subspace projection already preserves the protected structure.

The additional restriction may reduce attack strength without providing a corresponding downstream benefit.

## 7.4 SVD-Gap-Uncapped

The SVD-Gap-Uncapped method removes the ordinary maximum ε=3 cap from the gap-based complementary-subspace attack.

Its nominal parameters were:

\[
\epsilon=1000,\quad \alpha=200.
\]

Nevertheless, the mean effective perturbation budget was approximately:

\[
\epsilon_{\text{effective}}=2.3648.
\]

The attack remained constrained by the spectral gap.

SVD-Gap-Uncapped achieved:

- Clean R²: 0.6902
- Unrestricted PGD R²: 0.5422
- SVD-Gap attack R²: 0.6271
- 30% dropout R²: 0.4184

The slightly larger effective budget improved some average scores compared with capped SVD-Gap, but it did not improve severe neuron-dropout robustness.

This arm should be interpreted separately from the fixed-cap methods because its nominal perturbation budget differs substantially.

# 8. Evaluation of the New SVD-Split Method

## 8.1 Experimental Objective

SVD-Split was introduced to investigate whether combining tangent-space and complementary-subspace perturbations could provide a compromise between two previously observed behaviors:

- Complementary-subspace training tends to preserve dominant singular structure accurately and retain reasonable PGD robustness.
- Tangent-space training tends to preserve dropout tolerance but is highly vulnerable to unrestricted PGD.

SVD-Split attempts to combine both perturbation geometries while maintaining a spectral-gap certificate.

## 10.3 Spectral-Gap Preservation and Collapse Diagnostics

The updated fork includes improved diagnostics for the spectral separation of clean dominant directions and the complementary structure.

The diagnostic `gap_kept` compares the weakest clean dominant direction against the strongest clean-complement direction after perturbation, normalized by the original spectral gap.

A nonpositive value indicates that the complement has reached or exceeded the weakest protected dominant direction according to this criterion.

The diagnostic also reports a mode-exchange fraction based on whether the largest principal angle between clean and attacked leading subspaces exceeds 45°.

# 13. Discussion

## 13.1 Strongest Positive Result: SVD-Subspace

The most balanced result in the expanded experiment was obtained by SVD-Subspace.

It achieved:

- The highest mean clean decoding R².
- A small average advantage over PGD under unrestricted PGD evaluation.
- The best robustness against SVD-Gap attacks.
- The best Poisson-corruption result.
- The best 10% neuron-dropout result.
- The best gain-drift result.
- Numerical-precision preservation of the selected dominant singular structure.

The major limitation was severe neuron loss: SVD-Subspace performed poorly under 30% dropout.

Consequently, SVD-Subspace is a promising direction when the priority is improving decoding and maintaining resistance to moderate noise or norm-bounded attacks, but it should not be presented as uniformly robust to missing neural units.

## 13.2 Tangent Geometry Produces a Distinct Robustness Profile

The SVD-Tangent method retained substantially more performance under severe neuron loss than conventional PGD.

The matched-budget ablation indicates that this difference cannot be explained simply by the smaller average epsilon.

However, Clean CEBRA achieved nearly the same 30% dropout performance.

The evidence therefore supports a narrower but interesting conclusion:

**The tangent-space perturbation geometry appears to retain natural neuron-dropout tolerance while producing a specific, limited form of adversarial resistance.**

It does not deliver robust performance against unrestricted PGD.

The method's extremely low unrestricted-PGD R² indicates that it should not be used as a general-purpose adversarial defense without further modification.

## 13.3 SVD-Split Partially Recovers Adversarial Robustness

SVD-Split successfully combined nontrivial tangent and complementary perturbation components.

Its empirical performance was intermediate between conventional PGD and SVD-Tangent under the main robustness tests.

It recovered substantial unrestricted-PGD resistance relative to SVD-Tangent but retained only a modest fraction of the tangent method's neuron-dropout advantage.

This suggests that the joint perturbation geometry influences the trade-off, but the current configuration does not achieve a simultaneous improvement in both objectives.

Further work could examine alternative allocations of the tangent and complementary budgets.

However, such modifications should be evaluated with explicit control over the total perturbation norm and the resulting spectral certificate.

## 13.4 Why the Clean Baseline Matters

The inclusion of non-adversarial CEBRA significantly changes the interpretation of the project.

Without Clean CEBRA, the 30% neuron-dropout results could be interpreted as evidence that tangent adversarial training creates strong new dropout robustness.

With the Clean baseline, a different pattern becomes visible.

Conventional PGD training improves adversarial robustness but substantially reduces tolerance to missing neural units.

Tangent training avoids much of this reduction, while complementary-subspace training tends to retain adversarial robustness at the expense of severe neuron-dropout tolerance.

This suggests that adversarial training geometry influences how the encoder distributes or uses behaviorally relevant neural information.

Whether this occurs through increased representational redundancy, changes in feature reliance, or another mechanism remains an open question.

## 13.5 Spectral Preservation and General Robustness Are Distinct

The experimental results also show that preserving dominant singular values is not sufficient to guarantee robustness to every type of disturbance.

For example, SVD-Subspace preserved the dominant singular structure almost exactly but performed poorly under 30% neuron dropout.

Conversely, SVD-Tangent allowed greater changes in leading singular components while retaining relatively high neuron-dropout tolerance.

Therefore, the mathematical property of dominant spectral preservation and the practical property of robustness to recording degradation should be evaluated separately.

The project is better characterized as an investigation of **how spectral perturbation geometry shapes neural representations**, rather than simply as a method that guarantees improved adversarial robustness.

# 14. Limitations

Several limitations remain before the results can support broad scientific claims.

### 14.1 Single Recording Session

All experiments were performed on C-CO12.

The results have not yet been replicated across additional recording sessions, subjects, brain regions, or behavioral tasks.

### 14.2 Three Random Seeds

Three seeds provide an initial estimate of variation.

However, this sample size is insufficient for strong statistical conclusions, particularly when observed differences are small.

### 14.3 Unequal Effective Training Budgets

The SVD methods use different adaptive budgets.

Their performance can be influenced by both perturbation direction and magnitude.

Budget-only ablations address this issue partially, but additional PGD controls at ε≈2.23 and ε≈1.18 would improve the analysis.

### 14.4 SVD-Split Total Budget

SVD-Split uses two separately constrained components.

The experiment reports a mean combined perturbation norm below 3, but does not establish that the total perturbation norm is bounded by 3 in every window.

This matters when comparing the method with ordinary PGD under a fixed ε=3 constraint.

### 14.5 Simulated Neuron Loss

Neuron dropout was implemented by setting selected recording channels to zero.

This is a useful stress test but is not equivalent to observing real longitudinal loss of isolated neural units.

Additional experiments using actual cross-session recordings would strengthen the interpretation.

### 14.6 Biological Validity of Perturbations

The neural recordings originate from spike-count measurements.

The adversarial optimization operates in a continuous numerical space and does not necessarily preserve integer-valued, nonnegative spike counts.

Consequently, the perturbations should not yet be described as guaranteed physiologically realizable spike-count modifications.

### 14.7 Robustness Evaluation Coverage

The current results use the specified 20-step adversarial evaluation protocol.

They do not establish worst-case robustness against all possible attacks, nor do they constitute formal robustness certification of the trained encoder–decoder pipeline.

The spectral certificates concern properties of the perturbations and the selected singular structure, not certified bounds on prediction error.

# 15. Recommended Next Experiments

The following experiments would most directly strengthen the research.

**Matched-budget PGD controls.** Train conventional PGD with ε≈1.18 and ε≈2.23 and compare under identical evaluation attacks.

**Additional neural sessions.** Replicate the nine-method comparison on multiple recording sessions to assess whether the observed patterns are specific to C-CO12.

**Neuron-loss severity curves.** Evaluate dropout fractions from mild to severe, for example 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%.

**Structured neuron loss.** Compare random neuron dropout with removal of behaviorally informative or high-variance neural units.

**Perturbation-geometry analysis.** Quantify how realistic recording corruptions project into tangent and complementary spectral subspaces, using the new corruption-geometry analysis tools.

**Budget-normalized SVD-Split.** Evaluate variants with an explicit total perturbation-norm constraint while retaining the joint spectral certificate.

**Representation analysis.** Investigate the effects of the training methods on embedding geometry, feature importance, and redundancy of behavioral information.

**Additional robustness evaluation.** Test stronger and alternative adversarial attack procedures to reduce sensitivity to the choice of evaluation attack.

# 16. Conclusions

The expanded 27-run Acorn-SVD experiment provides a substantially clearer picture of the relationship between spectral perturbation geometry, neural representation quality, and robustness.

The most important conclusions are:

**1. Complementary-subspace-constrained attacks accurately preserve selected dominant singular structure.**

SVD-Gap, SVD-Gap-Uncapped, and SVD-Subspace reduced the maximum recorded relative leading singular-value errors to approximately numerical precision.

**2. SVD-Subspace is the strongest overall method in the current benchmark.**

It achieved the highest average clean decoding R² and the highest mean scores under the three adversarial evaluation conditions, together with strong performance under Poisson resampling, mild dropout, and gain drift.

Its advantage in unrestricted-PGD robustness over conventional PGD is small and not consistently positive across all seeds.

**3. Tangent-space training strongly affects robustness to severe neural unit loss.**

SVD-Tangent substantially outperformed conventional PGD under 30% neuron dropout.

However, it performed approximately as well as the non-adversarial Clean CEBRA baseline, indicating that its primary effect may be preserving natural dropout tolerance rather than creating a new level of robustness beyond clean training.

**4. Tangent-space training has a major adversarial robustness limitation.**

Its mean unrestricted-PGD R² was only 0.1972, much lower than the conventional PGD and SVD-Subspace models.

**5. SVD-Split produces an intermediate robustness compromise rather than eliminating the trade-off.**

It improved neuron-dropout robustness relative to conventional PGD and recovered substantial PGD robustness relative to SVD-Tangent, but it did not match the strongest models on both objectives.

**6. Perturbation geometry is a promising research variable in neural contrastive learning.**

The experiments show that adversarial training constraints influence different properties of the learned representation in distinct ways.

Overall, these findings support continued investigation of spectral-geometry-constrained adversarial learning as a way to study and control the robustness characteristics of neural representations.

The central scientific opportunity is not merely to improve one aggregate accuracy metric, but to explain **why different perturbation geometries induce different sensitivities to adversarial attacks and neural recording degradation**.

Demonstrating this relationship across multiple independent neural recording sessions, with matched-budget controls and mechanistic analysis, would provide a stronger foundation for a full research publication.
